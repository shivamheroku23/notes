## 2. 📚 Core Definitions & Full Forms

### Basic Definitions

1. **Percentage (%):**  
   A way of expressing a number as a **fraction of 100**.  
   \- Hindi: 100 ke ratio mein kisi value ko likhna → “percent”.

   \[
   \text{Percentage} = \frac{\text{Value}}{\text{Total}} \times 100\%
   \]

2. **Percentage Increase / Decrease:**

   \[
   \text{Percentage Change} = \frac{\text{New} - \text{Old}}{\text{Old}} \times 100\%
   \]

   - If result is **+ve → increase**  
   - If result is **–ve → decrease**

3. **Successive Percentage Change:**  
   When percentage change happens **more than once sequentially** (e.g., +20% then –10%).  
   Exact compound effect, not simple addition.

4. **Fraction–Percentage relationship:**  
   Common equivalents used in exams:

   | Fraction | Percentage |
   |----------|-----------:|
   | 1/2      | 50%        |
   | 1/3      | 33⅓%       |
   | 1/4      | 25%        |
   | 1/5      | 20%        |
   | 1/8      | 12.5%      |
   | 3/4      | 75%        |
   | 2/5      | 40%        |
   | 3/5      | 60%        |
   | 4/5      | 80%        |

5. **Base Value:**  
   The value on which percentage is calculated.  
   - 20% of **200** → base = 200.  
   - Misunderstanding base is the **#1 error** in exam questions.

> [!NOTE]
> Books like RS Aggarwal and Kiran define percentage similarly; **only base selection and algebra forms differ**.

## 3. 🧠 Deep Logic & Theory

### 3.1 Why Percentage Formula Works

Consider a class of 50 students. If 20 are girls:

- Fraction of girls = 20/50  
- To express out of 100, scale by 100:

\[
\frac{20}{50} \times 100 = 40\%
\]

So **percentage is just a scaled fraction**. The “/100” logic is:

\[
x\% = \frac{x}{100}
\]

This is why in questions we often treat “x%” as “x/100”.

---

### 3.2 Why Successive Percentages Are Not Simple Additions

If a quantity increases by **a%** then **b%**, final net percentage change is:

\[
\text{Net \% change} = a + b + \frac{ab}{100}
\]

**Derivation:**

Original value = 100 (assumption for easy math)

1. After a% increase:

\[
100 \to 100\left(1 + \frac{a}{100}\right) = 100 + a
\]

2. After b% increase on new value:

\[
(100 + a)\left(1 + \frac{b}{100}\right)
= (100 + a) + \frac{b}{100}(100 + a)
= 100 + a + b + \frac{ab}{100}
\]

Net increase over 100 = \(a + b + \dfrac{ab}{100}\)  

For decrease, signs change accordingly.

---

### 3.3 Relation Between Percentage & Ratio

If A’s value is **p% more** than B:

- A = B × (1 + p/100)
- Ratio A:B = (100 + p) : 100

If A is **p% less** than B:

- A = B × (1 – p/100)
- Ratio A:B = (100 – p) : 100

These forms are heavily used in **Profit–Loss, Population, Salary, Marks** questions.

> [!TIP]
> Convert **“more than / less than”** statements into ratios quickly. It reduces calculations in Mains-level questions.

## 4. ⚡ Algorithms / Step-wise Templates

We’ll cover **4 practical methods**:

1. **Method 1 – Direct Formula (Beginner Friendly)**
2. **Method 2 – Fraction & Ratio Method (Fast for Arithmetic)**
3. **Method 3 – Assumption of Convenient Base (100, 1000, etc.)**
4. **Method 4 – Net Percentage & Reverse Percentage (Mains Twist)**

---

### 4.1 Method 1 – Direct Formula

Best for: beginners, simple Prelims sums.

**Template:**

1. Identify **base value**.  
2. Apply formula: required value = (percentage/100) × base.  
3. For increase/decrease:  
   - New = Old × (1 ± p/100).

**Example:** 20% of 250?

\[
= \frac{20}{100} \times 250 = 50
\]

**Pros:**  
- Simple, easy to remember.  
**Cons:**  
- Slow when numbers are large/ugly; not ideal for Mains.

---

### 4.2 Method 2 – Fraction & Ratio Method

Best for: Profit–Loss, Discount, DI.

**Template:**

1. Convert given percentage into **fraction** (use memory table).  
2. Multiply with base using mental math.  

**Example:** 12.5% of 320?

- 12.5% = 1/8  
- So 1/8 of 320 = 40

**Pros:** **Very fast**, especially with common percentages.  
**Cons:** Requires good fraction-percentage memory.

---

### 4.3 Method 3 – Assume Convenient Base (100 Rule)

Best for: percentage comparison, data where actual total not needed.

**Template:**

1. Assume base = 100 (or LCM of percentages).  
2. Apply percentage changes on assumed base.  
3. Use resulting numbers to compare or find ratios.

**Example:** A scored 20% more than B. If difference is 30 marks, find B’s marks.

- Assume B = 100 → A = 120  
- Difference = 20 marks (on this scale) corresponds to 30 real marks  
  ⇒ Multiply by 30/20 = 1.5  
- So B = 100 × 1.5 = 150 marks

**Pros:**  
- Avoids equations; very fast in comparison problems.  
**Cons:**  
- Needs comfort with assumptions and scaling.

---

### 4.4 Method 4 – Net & Reverse Percentage (Mains-Oriented)

**Net Percentage:**

- For consecutive changes a% and b%:

\[
\text{Net \% change} = a + b + \frac{ab}{100}
\]

**Reverse Percentage (finding original):**

If final value after p% increase is **F**, then original **O**:

\[
O = \frac{F}{1 + p/100}
\]

If final is after p% decrease:

\[
O = \frac{F}{1 - p/100}
\]

**Pros:**  
- Essential for population, salary, price/discount chains.  
**Cons:**  
- Error-prone if signs are mishandled.

> [!WARNING]
> Many mains questions hide **reverse percentage** inside long English statements. Train yourself to “see” these patterns.

## 5. 📝 Practice Questions (with Detailed Solutions)

### Pattern

- **Q1–Q5:** Easy (Prelims)  
- **Q6–Q12:** Moderate  
- **Q13–Q20:** High-level / Mains-oriented mix  

Time assumptions (for good accuracy):
- Easy: **30–40 sec** each  
- Moderate: **45–60 sec**  
- Hard: **60–90+ sec**

---

### Q1 (Easy – Direct %)

A student scores **40%** marks in an exam of **250 marks**. How many marks did he score?

**Target time:** 20–25 sec

**Solution – Method 1 (Direct formula)**

Marks scored:

\[
= \frac{40}{100} \times 250
= 0.4 \times 250
= 100
\]

**Answer:** 100 marks.

**Solution – Method 2 (Fraction idea)**

- 40% = 2/5  
- 2/5 of 250 = (250 ÷ 5) × 2 = 50 × 2 = 100

**Analysis:**

- Classic **intro-level** question – seen in Kiran / previous Clerk papers.
- Trap: None, just speed check.

---

### Q2 (Easy – Percentage Increase)

The price of a pen increases from **₹20** to **₹25**. What is the percentage increase?

**Target time:** 25–30 sec

**Solution – Method 1 (Formula)**

Increase = 25 – 20 = 5

\[
\text{\% increase} = \frac{5}{20} \times 100 = 25\%
\]

**Solution – Method 2 (Ratio thinking)**

- Old : New = 20 : 25 = 4 : 5  
- Increase = 1 part over 4 → (1/4) = 25%

**Analysis:**

- Typical **prelims-level** pattern.
- Trap: Students sometimes mistakenly divide by **new** value (25) instead of **old** (20).

---

### Q3 (Easy – Percentage Decrease)

A shopkeeper reduces the price of a shirt from **₹800** to **₹640**. Find the percentage decrease.

**Target time:** 30 sec

Decrease = 800 – 640 = 160

\[
\text{\% decrease} = \frac{160}{800} \times 100
= \frac{16000}{800}
= 20\%
\]

Alternate:

- 800 → 640  
- Ratio = 5 : 4  
- Decrease = 1 part out of 5 → 20%

---

### Q4 (Easy – Fraction to %)

What percent of **64** is **24**?

**Target time:** 30 sec

\[
\text{\%} = \frac{24}{64} \times 100
= \frac{3}{8} \times 100
= 37.5\%
\]

Using fraction: 3/8 = 37.5% (memorized).

---

### Q5 (Easy – Result into %)

In a class of 40 students, 10 are absent. What percent of students are present?

**Target time:** 25–30 sec

Present = 40 – 10 = 30

\[
\text{\% present} = \frac{30}{40} \times 100
= \frac{3}{4} \times 100
= 75\%
\]

---

### Q6 (Moderate – Successive Increase)

The salary of a person is increased by **20%** and then again by **10%**. What is the net percentage increase in his salary?

**Target time:** 45–50 sec

**Method 1 – Net % formula**

\[
\text{Net \% change} = 20 + 10 + \frac{20 \times 10}{100}
= 30 + 2 = 32\%
\]

**Method 2 – Assume base**

Let salary = 100

- After 20% increase → 120  
- After 10% increase → 120 + 12 = 132  

Increase = 32 over 100 → 32%

**Answer:** 32% net increase.

**Analysis:**

- Pattern similar to many **IBPS PO / SBI PO mains arithmetic** questions.
- Trap: Adding 20 + 10 = 30 (ignoring compound effect).

---

### Q7 (Moderate – Successive Decrease)

The price of a commodity is decreased by **20%** and then further decreased by **10%**. What is the overall decrease?

**Target time:** 45–60 sec

Using formula:

\[
\text{Net \% change} = -20 + (-10) + \frac{(-20)(-10)}{100}
= -30 + 2 = -28\%
\]

So **28% net decrease**.

Alternative: Assume 100 → 80 → 72  
Decrease = 28.

---

### Q8 (Moderate – Reverse %)

After getting **25%** increment, a person’s salary becomes **₹50,000**. What was his salary before increment?

**Target time:** 45–60 sec

**Method 1 – Reverse % formula**

\[
\text{Original} = \frac{\text{Final}}{1 + \frac{25}{100}}
= \frac{50000}{1.25}
= 50000 \times \frac{100}{125}
= 50000 \times \frac{4}{5}
= 40000
\]

**Method 2 – Ratio**

- 25% increase → new:old = 125 : 100  
- If 125 parts = 50,000 → 1 part = 400  
- Old = 100 parts = 40,000  

**Answer:** ₹40,000.

---

### Q9 (Moderate – Relation/Comparison)

A’s salary is **20% more** than B’s salary. By what percent is B’s salary less than A’s?

**Target time:** 45 sec

Assume B = 100 → A = 120

Difference = 20  

As a % of A:

\[
\frac{20}{120} \times 100 = \frac{1}{6} \times 100 \approx 16.67\%
\]

**Answer:** B’s salary is approximately **16⅔%** less than A’s.

**Analysis:**

- Frequently asked in PO/Clerk – inspired by Kiran question bank.
- Trap: Many mark 20% (same figure) which is wrong.

---

### Q10 (Moderate – Election Type)

In an election between two candidates, the winner gets **55%** of the valid votes and wins by a margin of **2,000 votes**. How many valid votes were cast?

**Target time:** 60 sec

Winner: 55%, Loser: 45%

Difference = 10% ≡ 2,000 votes

\[
1\% = \frac{2000}{10} = 200
\]

Total valid votes = 100% = 200 × 100 = 20,000

**Answer:** 20,000 valid votes.

**Alternative (ratio):**  
55% : 45% → 11 : 9; difference 2 parts = 2,000 → 1 part = 1,000 → total 20 parts = 20,000.

---

### Q11 (Moderate – Population)

The population of a town increases by **10%** in the first year and by **20%** in the second year. If current population is 39,600, what was the population 2 years ago?

**Target time:** 70–80 sec

Let original population = P.

After 1st year: P × 1.10  
After 2nd year: P × 1.10 × 1.20 = P × 1.32

Given: P × 1.32 = 39,600

\[
P = \frac{39600}{1.32} = \frac{39600 \times 100}{132}
= \frac{3,960,000}{132}
= 30,000
\]

**Answer:** 30,000.

**Net % check:**  
Net % = 10 + 20 + (10×20)/100 = 30 + 2 = 32%  
So 132% of P = 39,600 → P = 30,000 (same result).

---

### Q12 (Moderate – Discount Connection)

The marked price of a shirt is **₹1,500**. A shopkeeper offers a **20% discount** and still gains **20% profit**. Find the cost price.

**Target time:** 60–75 sec

Let MP = 1,500. Discount 20% → SP:

\[
SP = 1500 \times (1 - 0.20) = 1500 \times 0.8 = 1200
\]

SP is 20% above CP:

\[
SP = CP \times 1.20
\Rightarrow 1200 = 1.2 \times CP
\Rightarrow CP = \frac{1200}{1.2} = 1000
\]

**Answer:** ₹1,000.

---

### Q13 (High – Mains Style, Net % with Mix)

A shopkeeper increases the price of a product by **25%** but then allows a discount of **20%** on the increased price. What is his net gain or loss percent with respect to the original price?

**Target time:** 60–75 sec

Assume original price = 100.

After 25% increase → 125.  
After 20% discount on 125 → SP:

\[
SP = 125 \times (1 - 0.20) = 125 \times 0.8 = 100
\]

So final SP = 100 = original price.

**Net gain/loss:** 0%.

**Using formula:**

Successive changes: +25% and –20%.

\[
\text{Net \% change} = 25 - 20 + \frac{25 \times (-20)}{100}
= 5 - 5 = 0\%
\]

**Analysis:**

- This exact pattern often appears in **Profit–Loss** chapter.  
- Very common trap: students say “5% gain”, ignoring compound effect.

---

### Q14 (High – Students/Marks)

In an exam, a student scored **20%** marks and failed by **15 marks**. Another student scored **32%** marks and got **21 marks** more than the pass marks. Find the maximum marks of the exam.

**Target time:** 70–90 sec

Let total marks = M, pass marks = P.

First student: 20% of M = P – 15  
Second student: 32% of M = P + 21  

Subtract first from second:

\[
0.32M - 0.20M = (P + 21) - (P - 15)
\Rightarrow 0.12M = 36
\Rightarrow M = \frac{36}{0.12} = 300
\]

**Answer:** 300 maximum marks.

**Pattern:**  
This is a **classic bank exam style** sum often in Kiran/RS Aggarwal.

---

### Q15 (High – DI-Flavored Single Q)

In a company, 40% of employees work in the **Sales** department and 30% of the remaining work in **HR**. If the number of employees in HR is 180, find the total number of employees in the company.

**Target time:** 60–80 sec

Let total employees = T.

Sales = 40% of T → remaining = 60% of T.

HR = 30% of remaining = 0.30 × 0.60 T = 0.18 T

Given HR = 180 → 0.18 T = 180

\[
T = \frac{180}{0.18} = 1000
\]

**Answer:** 1,000 employees.

**Alternative – Fraction:**  
40% = 2/5, remaining = 3/5 T.  
30% of 3/5 T = 3/10 T.  
3/10 T = 180 → T = 600 (this shows our earlier decimal is wrong!).  

Re-check decimal approach:

- Remaining after 40% sales = 60% = 0.6 T  
- 30% of 0.6 T = 0.18 T  
If 0.18 T = 180 → T = 1000 (correct by decimals).

Let’s check fraction:

40% = 2/5, remaining = 3/5 T.  
30% of remaining = (30/100) × (3/5 T) = 3/10 × 3/5 T = 9/50 T.

So:

\[
\frac{9}{50} T = 180
\Rightarrow T = 180 \times \frac{50}{9} = 1000
\]

So **final correct total = 1000**.

> [!WARNING]
> Be careful with percentage-to-fraction steps. Small slip (3/10 instead of 9/50) can change answer.

### Q16 (High – Mains, Reverse Chain)

The population of a village increased by **10%** in the first year, decreased by **5%** in the second year, and again increased by **20%** in the third year. If the current population is **13,860**, what was the population three years ago?

**Target time:** 80–90 sec

Let initial population = P.

After 1st year: P × 1.10  
After 2nd year: P × 1.10 × 0.95  
After 3rd year: P × 1.10 × 0.95 × 1.20

Current:

\[
P \times 1.10 \times 0.95 \times 1.20 = 13860
\]

Compute factor:

1.10 × 0.95 = 1.045  
1.045 × 1.20 = 1.254  

So:

\[
P \times 1.254 = 13860
\Rightarrow P = \frac{13860}{1.254} \approx 11050\ (\text{exactly } 11050)
\]

**Answer:** 11,050.

**Pattern:** Mains-style population chain, inspired by common bank exam questions.

### Q17 (High – Profit-Linked %)

A shopkeeper sells an article at a discount of **10%** on the marked price but still gains **20%** on the cost price. If the marked price is **₹1,800**, find the cost price.

**Target time:** 70–90 sec

Discount 10% on MP 1,800:

\[
SP = 1800 \times 0.9 = 1620
\]

SP = 120% of CP.

\[
CP = \frac{SP}{1.20}
= \frac{1620}{1.2}
= 1350
\]

**Answer:** ₹1,350.

---

### Q18 (High – Exam-Level, Salary Reverse)

A person spends **80%** of his income. His income increases by **25%**, and his expenditure increases by **20%**. By what percentage do his savings increase?

**Target time:** 80–90 sec

Assume original income = 100.  
Original expenditure = 80 → savings = 20.

New income = 100 × 1.25 = 125  
New expenditure = 80 × 1.20 = 96  
New savings = 125 – 96 = 29

Increase in savings = 29 – 20 = 9

\[
\text{\% increase in savings} = \frac{9}{20} \times 100 = 45\%
\]

**Answer:** 45% increase.

---

### Q19 (High – Quantity Comparison Style)

**Quantity I:** 40% of a number is 84.  
**Quantity II:** 30% of the same number is 63.  

Which is greater?

**Target time:** 40–50 sec

Let number = N.

From QI: 0.40N = 84 → N = 210  
From QII: 0.30N = 63 → N = 210

Both give same N.

Now QI = 84, QII = 63 → QI > QII.

**Answer:** Quantity I is greater.

### Q20 (High – DI Link, Caselet Style)

In a company, **60%** of employees are male. **40%** of males and **20%** of females are graduates. If the number of non-graduate employees is **360**, find the total number of employees.

**Target time:** 90 sec

Let total employees = T.

Males = 0.6T → Females = 0.4T

Graduate males = 40% of 0.6T = 0.24T  
Graduate females = 20% of 0.4T = 0.08T  

Total graduates = 0.24T + 0.08T = 0.32T  

Non-graduates = T – 0.32T = 0.68T  

Given 0.68T = 360

\[
T = \frac{360}{0.68} = \frac{36000}{68} = \frac{9000}{17} \approx 529.41
\]

This is not an integer → likely mis-assumption.

Re-check: maybe numbers in exam would give integer. Let’s adjust thinking:

Non-graduates:

- Male non-graduate = 60% – 40% of 60% = 60% – 24% = 36% of T  
- Female non-graduate = 40% – 20% of 40% = 40% – 8% = 32% of T  
Total non-graduate = 36% + 32% = 68% of T → 0.68T = 360 → T ≈ 529.41

In many real exam questions, numbers are chosen to give integer results; here, treat 360 as inspired data.  

For integer neatness, if non-grads were 340 (instead of 360):

\[
0.68T = 340
\Rightarrow T = 500
\]

> [!NOTE]
> Real exam questions keep values divisible. The logic above is what matters; in mocks/PYQs, numbers will be adjusted to fit an integer total.

## 6. 🕳️ Common Traps & Mistakes

1. **Wrong Base Selection**  
   - Saying “marks increased from 40 to 50 → 10/50 = 20%” (wrong).  
   - Correct: 10/40 = 25% (base is **original**).

2. **Adding Successive Percentages Naively**  
   - +20% and –10% ≠ +10%.  
   - Net = 20 – 10 – (20×10)/100 = 10 – 2 = 8%.

3. **Confusing ‘More Than’ vs ‘Of’**  
   - A is 25% more than B → A = 1.25B.  
   - But 25% of B is only 0.25B.

4. **Ignoring Reverse Percentage**  
   - “After increasing by 25%, salary became 50,000” – students try linear thinking instead of dividing by 1.25.

5. **Misreading Question Language (English trap)**  
   - “How much percent is A of B?” vs “How much percent more than B?” – one is **simple ratio**, other is **difference over base**.
